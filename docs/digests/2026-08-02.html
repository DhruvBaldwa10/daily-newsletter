LLMs Predict Science, Retail Agents Ship

A Nature paper says LLMs can predict social science experiment outcomes better than humans. OpenAI's GPT-Realtime hit 30,000 retail shoppers in two weeks. And the infrastructure layer is quietly getting rebuilt from scratch — INT4 memory cells, ternary weights, CPU-native long-context encoders.

LLMs as Social Scientists: A Nature Paper Worth Arguing About

A paper published this week in Nature makes a claim that will either reshape how you think about LLM capabilities or send you hunting for methodological holes: large language models can predict the results of social science experiments — including which hypotheses will replicate — with accuracy that exceeds human experts. The study covers a range of classic behavioral economics and psychology experimental paradigms and finds that the models outperform researchers who read the same abstracts and study designs.

The obvious rejoinder is that frontier LLMs trained on the scientific literature could be pattern-matching against known results, not reasoning about experimental design. That critique has real weight. But the paper controls for it in several ways, and even a skeptical reading leaves something uncomfortable standing: the models are at least as good as domain experts at predicting which findings hold up, and they're doing it in seconds.

What's more interesting for product builders is the implied capability: if a model can evaluate whether an experimental design will produce a reliable result, it can also help you pressure-test your own A/B test designs, user research plans, and eval datasets before you run them. That's a specific, useful application that doesn't require believing the strongest claims in the abstract.

"The unprecedented capabilities of foundation models have opened the door to agentic applications that were previously unimaginable."

— Chip Huyen, Agents

The broader question this raises for evals designers: if LLMs can predict human experimental outcomes, what does that mean for human-preference benchmarks that assume human judgment is the ground truth? That loop might be tighter than anyone wants to admit.

GPT-Realtime's First Real Deployment Numbers

OpenAI dropped a case study this week that's worth reading past the marketing framing: avatarin deployed GPT-Realtime in a Yamada Denki retail environment and hit 30,000 users in two weeks, with 92% positive survey responses. Yamada Denki is Japan's largest electronics retailer — think Best Buy, scaled — so the floor traffic is real, not a curated pilot cohort.

The 24/7 multilingual support angle matters more than the headline numbers. Japanese retail has historically required human staff fluent in multiple Asian languages to serve inbound tourist shoppers; GPT-Realtime removes that constraint entirely. Avatarin's underlying product is a telepresence robot platform, so this is a voice-native, real-time, embodied deployment — not a chat widget people opt into on a website.

Two weeks, 30,000 users, 92% positive. That's a pass rate that most enterprise software teams would celebrate after a year of tuning. It also reflects how much better real-time voice models have gotten at handling ambient noise, speaker switching, and topic drift in retail environments — problems that killed earlier voice assistant deployments.

"In two weeks, 30,000 people used the agent and 92% of survey responses were positive."

— OpenAI Blog, avatarin case study

The deployment model here — physical robot as the interface, cloud LLM as the brain — suggests the avatar-as-agent pattern is maturing faster than most people assumed. If you're building in retail, hospitality, or any physical-world service context and haven't seriously priced GPT-Realtime API costs into your 2027 roadmap, the timeline may be shorter than your planning assumptions.

The Inference Stack Is Being Rebuilt Underneath You

Three distinct infrastructure projects surfaced today that, taken together, represent a systematic attack on the cost and latency assumptions baked into current LLM deployments. None of them are production-ready, but all three are pointing at the same problem from different angles.

First: a preprint on persistent state machines using INT4 in-memory cells for LLM attention proposes replacing the stateless KV cache model with an in-memory state machine that persists across requests. The claim is significantly reduced memory bandwidth consumption for multi-turn workloads — which is exactly where inference costs compound. Second: Tritium, an open-source ternary-model inference and training framework targeting CUDA, CPU, and BitNet/TQ formats, aims to make 1.58-bit weight models practical without dedicated hardware. Third: Liquid AI published details on LFM2.5 encoders optimized for fast long-context inference on CPU — no GPU required.

The Liquid AI piece is the most immediately actionable. If you're building any product that needs long-context inference at the edge — on-device, air-gapped, or in cost-sensitive enterprise environments — CPU-native long-context is a different deployment conversation than it was six months ago.

"From Gemma 4 to DeepSeek V4, How New Open-Weight LLMs Are Reducing Long-Context Costs."

— Sebastian Raschka, Recent Developments in LLM Architectures

The SemiAnalysis analysis of H100 vs. GB200 NVL72 TCO remains the best frame for understanding why this bottom-up rebuilding is happening: Blackwell rack economics are forcing operators to rethink workload routing, and that pressure is flowing down into inference software.

Evals Are Getting More Honest About What They Test

Simon Willison shipped smevals, a small, deliberately narrow eval suite built with Prime Radiant's applied AI research lab. The design philosophy is worth noticing: rather than trying to build comprehensive capability benchmarks, smevals targets specific, answerable questions about model and prompt behavior in real harnesses. Small surface area, honest scope.

This lands the same week that the AI Snake Oil team's CRUX project — which goes in the opposite direction, targeting long, messy, open-world tasks — is gaining visibility. The contrast is instructive. CRUX is trying to measure what models actually do when the task is underspecified and multi-step. Smevals is trying to catch regressions and prompt-sensitivity bugs in specific production harnesses. Both exist because the middle ground — MMLU-style academic benchmarks — has become nearly useless for product decisions.

Hamel Husain's evals-skills framework, published in March after working with 50+ companies and 4,000+ students, captures the same frustration from the practitioner side. The common failure mode he documents: teams evaluating the wrong thing because they haven't looked at enough real traces to know what the model is actually getting wrong.

"Most AI teams focus on the wrong things."

— Hamel Husain, A Field Guide to Rapidly Improving AI Products

Eugene Yan's parallel work on evaluating long-context Q&A systems adds the benchmark-specific layer: for retrieval-heavy products, precision and recall on answer attribution matter more than any aggregate score, and building the right eval dataset for your specific retrieval setup is non-negotiable. The field is slowly converging on a stack where you need three things: a small, fast regression suite (smevals-style), a human-trace-grounded failure taxonomy (Hamel's approach), and at least one hard open-world benchmark to avoid overfitting your prompt engineering to your own evals.

Enterprise Lock-In Risk Is Moving Up the Stack

Arvind Narayanan published a piece at NormalTech arguing that AI's escape from the commodity trap is creating a new enterprise lock-in risk — not at the model layer, but at the workflow and integration layer above it. The argument: as base model prices collapse (see the GPT-5.6 pricing story from earlier this week), the business value — and the stickiness — is migrating to whoever controls the orchestration layer, the fine-tuned vertical models, and the proprietary data pipelines. Critics who say AI is a commodity and boosters who say it's defensible are both pointing at the wrong layer.

This maps directly to the Eugene Yan framework published in May: context as infrastructure, taste as config, verification for autonomy, scale via delegation. The companies building durable advantages right now aren't the ones with the best model access — it's the ones assembling proprietary context pipelines that make their AI meaningfully better on their specific tasks than any out-of-the-box deployment could be.

"Critics and boosters are both looking in the wrong place."

— Arvind Narayanan, Up the Stack: How AI's Escape From the Commodity Trap Risks Enterprise Lock-in

The Walsh project on HN — a multi-agent research pipeline with a risk manager that can veto trades — is a small but concrete example of this dynamic. The model choice is almost irrelevant; what matters is the veto logic, the risk parameterization, and the domain-specific data the agents are reasoning over. That's where the IP lives. If you're building enterprise AI products and haven't mapped which parts of your system would survive a model swap, that's the design exercise worth doing this quarter.

What This All Means

  1. The avatarin/GPT-Realtime deployment — 30,000 users, 92% satisfaction, two weeks — is the first large-scale public data point for real-time voice agents in physical retail. If your roadmap includes any voice-native or embodied agent use case, this is the benchmark to beat and the integration pattern to study.
  2. Three independent infrastructure projects (INT4 state machines, ternary inference, CPU-native long-context encoders) are converging on the same goal: making serious inference possible without current-generation GPU infrastructure. None is production-ready today, but the trajectory is clear enough to factor into your 18-month platform bets, especially for edge or cost-sensitive enterprise deployments.
  3. The evals field is splitting cleanly into two tools you need simultaneously: a small, fast regression suite for catching prompt-sensitivity regressions in your specific harness, and an open-world benchmark (CRUX or equivalent) to avoid overfitting your prompt engineering to your own tests. Treating them as alternatives rather than complements is the mistake most teams are making.
  4. As base model prices keep falling, the durable value in AI products is accumulating at the orchestration, context pipeline, and domain fine-tuning layers — not the model itself. Map which parts of your system survive a model swap; the parts that don't are where your defensibility actually lives.
